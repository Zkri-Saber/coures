Your resume has one job: to get you an interview. It isn't your life story. It's a short, focused argument that you can do *this* job well. In this lesson you'll learn how to build that argument, tailor it to each role, and make sure both software and people can read it.

## How your resume is read

Your resume usually has two readers, and you need to win over both.

**1. The software.** Many employers collect applications in an **applicant tracking system (ATS)**: software that stores resumes, pulls out details like job titles and dates, and lets recruiters search and filter candidates. If the software can't read your resume properly, a person may never see it.

**2. The person.** When a recruiter does open your resume, they skim it fast. In a 2018 eye-tracking study by the job site Ladders, recruiters spent an average of about **7 seconds** on their first look at a resume, mostly scanning job titles, employers and dates.

So your resume needs to be **easy for software to read** and **quick for a human to scan**.

## Before you write: research the job

Don't start with a blank page. Start with the job you want.

1. **Find two or three real job postings** for the kind of role you're aiming for.
2. **Highlight the skills and requirements** that appear again and again: tools, qualifications, soft skills, responsibilities.
3. **List your evidence.** For each highlighted item, write down where you've shown it. Experience counts from anywhere: jobs, internships, school or university projects, volunteering, clubs, sports teams, freelance work, helping in a family business.

This list becomes the raw material for everything else.

## The sections employers expect

Use a simple, familiar order. Recruiters know where to look, and tracking software recognises standard section names.

| Section | What goes in it |
| --- | --- |
| **Contact details** | Name, phone, a professional email, city and country, and a LinkedIn or portfolio link if you have one |
| **Summary** | Two or three lines about who you are, what you're good at, and the role you're aiming for |
| **Experience** | Your roles, newest first: job title, organisation, location, dates, and 3 to 5 bullet points each |
| **Projects** | School, personal or volunteer projects that prove relevant skills. Very useful when you have little work experience |
| **Education** | Qualification, school or university, dates, and relevant courses or grades if they're strong |
| **Skills** | Tools, technical skills and languages, matched to the job posting |

Optional extras: certifications, awards, volunteering, languages.

> Customs differ between countries. In some places a photo or date of birth is normal; in others it's discouraged. Follow the norms where you're applying, and always follow the employer's instructions.

## Write achievements, not duties

This is the biggest upgrade most resumes need. A **duty** says what you were supposed to do. An **achievement** says what you actually did and what changed because of it.

A reliable pattern is:

```text
Action verb  +  what you did  +  the result (with a number if you can)
```

Some people call this the **XYZ formula**: *accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z*.

| Duty (weak) | Achievement (strong) |
| --- | --- |
| Responsible for social media | Grew the club's Instagram from 200 to 850 followers in one term by posting three short videos a week |
| Helped customers in the shop | Served 60+ customers per shift and resolved complaints calmly, earning "employee of the month" twice |
| Did a group project on recycling | Led a team of 4 to survey 120 students; our proposal cut classroom waste by 30% in one month |

Tips for strong bullets:

- **Start with a strong verb:** led, built, organised, improved, designed, trained, reduced, launched.
- **Add numbers:** people, money, time saved, percentages, rankings, frequency. Estimates are fine if they're honest ("about 50 customers a day").
- **Keep each bullet to one or two lines.**
- **Put the most relevant bullets first** for the job you're applying to.

## Show your transferable skills

**Transferable skills** are abilities you can take from one situation to another: communication, teamwork, problem-solving, time management, leadership, customer service, adaptability.

Don't just list them ("Excellent communicator"). Anyone can type that. **Prove** them in your bullets:

- Weak: *Strong teamwork skills.*
- Strong: *Coordinated a 6-person volunteer team to run a weekend book sale that raised $1,200 for the school library.*

This matters most when you're changing fields or applying for your first job. Your café job or student club may have taught you exactly what an office or tech role needs.

## Tailor it for every application

Sending the same resume everywhere is the most common mistake. Instead:

1. Keep a **master resume** with everything you've ever done.
2. For each application, make a copy and **adjust it to the posting**:
   - Rewrite the summary for this role.
   - Reorder bullets so the most relevant come first.
   - Use the posting's own words for skills you really have. If the posting says "data analysis" and you wrote "worked with numbers", change it.
   - Remove things that don't help for this job.

> Never claim a skill or experience you don't have. Keywords might get you through software, but you'll be asked about every line in an interview.

## Format it so software can read it

Fancy designs often confuse tracking software. Career advisers commonly recommend:

- **One column.** Multiple columns can scramble the reading order.
- **No tables, text boxes, or text inside images.** Their contents may be skipped or jumbled.
- **Nothing important in the page header or footer.** Some systems ignore them, so put your contact details in the main body.
- **Standard section names:** "Experience", "Education", "Skills", not "My Journey".
- **Consistent dates**, like `Jun 2024 – Present` or `06/2024 – 08/2025`, used the same way everywhere.
- **A clear, common font** at 10 to 12 points, with plenty of white space.
- **The file type the employer asks for.** If they don't say, a simple PDF or Word file is usually fine. Name it clearly: `Sara-Ahmed-Resume.pdf`.

**Quick test:** copy all the text from your resume and paste it into a plain text editor. If it comes out jumbled, with sections mixed up or details missing, tracking software will probably struggle too.

## Use AI tools wisely

AI writing assistants can genuinely help:

- **Brainstorm** stronger verbs and different ways to phrase a bullet.
- **Compare** your resume with a job posting and spot missing keywords.
- **Tighten** long sentences and catch grammar mistakes.

But stay in charge:

- **Never let AI invent** experience, skills, numbers or qualifications. Check every word.
- **Keep your own voice.** Generic, robotic phrases ("results-driven synergistic team player") make you sound like everyone else.
- **Protect your privacy.** Remove your phone number, address and other personal details before pasting your resume into an online tool.

A good prompt to try: *"Here is a bullet from my resume and a job posting. Suggest three ways to rewrite the bullet so it shows results and uses the posting's language, without adding anything I didn't say."*

## Length and final polish

- **One page** is right for students and people early in their careers. Two pages is fine with many years of relevant experience.
- **Proofread** slowly, then read it aloud. Then ask someone else to read it: we all miss our own mistakes.
- **Check consistency:** same date style, same bullet style, same tense (past tense for past roles, present for current ones).
- **Make your email professional.** `sara.ahmed@...` beats `cutesara2009@...`.

## Checklist before you send

- [ ] Contact details in the main body, with a professional email
- [ ] Summary written for this specific job
- [ ] Bullets start with action verbs and show results
- [ ] Keywords from the posting appear naturally, and every claim is true
- [ ] One column, standard headings, no tables or text boxes
- [ ] Passed the plain-text paste test
- [ ] Proofread by you and one other person
- [ ] Saved in the requested file type with a clear file name
